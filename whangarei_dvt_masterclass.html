Advanced workshop · From popliteal fossa to mid-calf

Treat each view as a location to prove, rather than a pattern to memorise. You should be able to return to the same vessel after lifting the probe.

1 · Establish your map before chasing branches

State the side, probe marker direction and patient position. Locate the popliteal artery and vein in transverse view; show the vein collapsing. Follow the venous channels distally with small movements. At each branch, stop and identify which channel you are keeping on screen. A tributary disappearing out of plane does not mean it ends there. Use short longitudinal views to resolve direction, then return to transverse compression.

Reset when lost: return to the last unequivocal landmark. Find the artery again, lower pressure so adjacent veins can refill, and follow in shorter steps. Naming a vessel from one isolated still is less reliable than demonstrating its course.

2 · The medial mid-calf window

With the knee relaxed, approach from the medial calf. Identify the tibia and deeper fibula as orientation landmarks. Find the posterior tibial artery and inspect the accompanying venous channels. Search deeper toward the fibula for the peroneal artery and its veins. The peroneal system is commonly harder to resolve; alter the angle and depth rather than assuming the first deep vessel is peroneal. Confirm by tracking along its course. [1]

Use colour briefly to locate the arterial beacon. Then inspect the adjacent channels in greyscale before and during compression. Screen position varies with the window and marker orientation: avoid teaching a universal “left-hand pair” or “bottom pair”.

3 · Prove every companion channel

A compressible vein beside an artery does not clear its companion. Inspect every visible venous lumen and follow duplicated channels individually. Demonstrate adequate pressure and full wall apposition; if the target slides out of plane, reposition and repeat rather than recording disappearance as compression. [12]

4 · Deliberately leave the arterial bundle

The muscular-vein search is a separate sweep. Examine the gastrocnemius heads and the deeper soleus, including focal pain. Gastrocnemius drainage often leads toward the popliteal vein; soleal channels form a more variable intramuscular network. Trace suspicious channels to their outflow where visible. Do not call them superficial simply because they are outside the posterior tibial/peroneal bundle. [1] [2]

6 · Document a finding so the next clinician can act

Record the named vein, side, axial or muscular location, involved channels, compression abnormality, clot extent and proximal endpoint. Document deep-junction involvement, the proximal examination and all technical gaps. Save labelled compression loops and views mapping the endpoint. For SSV thrombus, record both length and distance along the vessel to the demonstrated SPJ.

Keep the two follow-up questions separate: a negative limited POCUS scan needs a diagnostic pathway to exclude unexamined disease; a confirmed untreated distal DVT needs a surveillance plan to detect extension. They are different indications for repeat imaging.

Bedside teaching cases · Reveal the discussion

One posterior tibial channel collapses; the adjacent channel does not. Is the bundle clear?

No. Paired or duplicated veins can have clot in only one channel. Demonstrate the abnormality, map its extent and obtain comprehensive duplex if identified on limited POCUS.

The deepest vessels have no colour, but you cannot apply adequate compression. Is this peroneal DVT?

The study is indeterminate in that segment. Re-optimise and obtain another window; escalate for formal duplex if unresolved. Do not diagnose clot from absent colour alone or exclude it from a normal neighbouring vessel.

A painful intramuscular channel is non-compressible while popliteal and posterior tibial veins appear normal.

Consider muscular distal DVT. Identify gastrocnemius versus soleal location, trace drainage, map extent and confirm the whole-leg picture. Treatment follows symptoms, extension risk, bleeding risk and the local pathway, not the anatomical label alone.

Supervisor observation checklist

  • Names the examination scope and identifies veins through their course.
  • Demonstrates compression in all assessed companion channels.
  • Separately searches axial and muscular systems.
  • Recognises technical limitations without overcalling a negative study.
  • Maps suspicious findings and states the next imaging or management step.

Start here: the five rules that prevent confusion

1. Think tributaries, not a perfect trifurcation

The popliteal vein commonly continues into the anterior tibial and tibioperoneal systems, while the latter divides into posterior tibial and peroneal veins. Real venous anatomy is variable, frequently paired or plexiform. [1]

2. Muscular veins are still deep veins

Gastrocnemius and soleal thrombosis is isolated distal DVT, not superficial thrombophlebitis. Its management can differ from an axial calf clot, but it must be documented. [2]

3. The anterior tibial veins are not ‘irrelevant’

They are deep axial calf veins, often technically less accessible and an uncommon site of isolated DVT. Assess them when clinically indicated or required by the local comprehensive protocol; the AIUM minimum compression list names posterior tibial and peroneal veins rather than mandating routine anterior tibial assessment. Assess focal symptoms deliberately. [1] [5]

4. The SSV–popliteal connection changes the stakes

Small saphenous thrombosis may be superficial, but close to the saphenopopliteal junction (SPJ) it can extend into the deep system. Precisely measure the distance to the junction; the junction itself is variable. [6] [7]
Do not confuse study types: a negative two-point/proximal POCUS examination does not exclude isolated calf DVT, iliac DVT, or technically unseen segments. The report must say what was actually examined. Use formal whole-leg duplex or an agreed serial-proximal strategy when the clinical pathway requires it.

2. A reproducible calf scan: follow the artery, then hunt the muscle sinuses

Comprehensive imaging technique This is an educational extension beyond limited ED proximal compression POCUS. Require training, image storage, and a pathway to credentialled whole-leg duplex.

  1. Patient position: prone if tolerated, or side-lying/seated with knee mildly flexed and calf relaxed. For posterior calf, use a small amount of reverse Trendelenburg/dependency to distend veins if needed. Avoid aggressive pressure before anatomy is established.
  2. Begin at the popliteal fossa: transverse view, vein generally more superficial than artery. Compress sequentially and follow distally through the bifurcating system; do not assume one named branch arrangement.
  3. Track tibioperoneal trunk → posterior tibial: use the adjacent artery as a beacon; trace vein pairs to medial ankle where practical. Compression every ~1–2 cm in the transverse plane with coverage of the entire identified segment. Do not confuse arterial walls with thrombus.
  4. Track peroneal veins: move posterolaterally/deeper toward fibula. Lower probe frequency/depth adjustment may be needed; colour Doppler is a locator, not a substitute for compression.
  5. Search medial/lateral gastrocnemius: sweep within muscle belly beneath superficial fascia, identify draining veins and track cranially to the popliteal confluence. Thrombosed veins can masquerade as hypoechoic muscle clefts.
  6. Search soleus: more distal/deep muscular channels; inspect the sinuses and their connection to axial veins. Use a broad sweep rather than expecting a single paired vessel.
  7. Anterior tibial: deliberate anterior/lateral window following anterior tibial artery; technically different from posterior calf study and included according to symptoms and the formal duplex protocol, rather than assumed to be part of every minimum examination.
  8. Trace SSV: posterior midline under superficial fascia upward to actual termination; note variations and any thrombus entering popliteal vein.

What makes a vein normal?

Simplified transverse compression concept; not a real sonogram. See published real ultrasound images below.
  • Complete, documented compressibility is the central sign.
  • Show vessel before and during compression; preserve short cine loops where available.
  • Acute thrombus may be hypoechoic; absence of bright clot is not reassuring.
  • Colour filling alone does not exclude non-occlusive thrombus; inadequate colour is not diagnostic either.
  • Report any unseen segment; a technically limited calf scan is not negative.
Missed clot trap: if the calf hurts focally but all examined axial channels compress, scan the painful muscle bed for gastrocnemius/soleal thrombosis and mimics (ruptured Baker cyst, haematoma, muscle tear). Ultrasound interpretation must match the clinical distribution.

3. What to do with an isolated calf DVT

First establish whether thrombus is truly below the popliteal vein, which vessels are involved, proximity to the popliteal system, symptoms, provoking factors, cancer/VTE history and bleeding risk. Anatomic location alone does not dictate treatment.

Lower symptoms / lower extension risk

Serial venous ultrasound once weekly for 2 weeks (and earlier if symptoms worsen) is an evidence-supported option. If there is no extension, anticoagulation is generally not required in the CHEST serial-imaging pathway. Surveillance must actually be arranged and reliable. [3]

Severe symptoms or extension risk factors

Anticoagulation is generally favoured if bleeding risk is acceptable; often 3 months when treating. Examples: active malignancy, previous VTE, unprovoked clot, significant size/burden, axial vein involvement or clot close to popliteal vein. [2] [3]

Risk assessment checklist

  • Burden: symptom severity, extent/length, multiple veins, diameter (large e.g. >7 mm), near-popliteal location
  • Host: active cancer, prior VTE, hospitalised/immobile, no reversible provoking factor, elevated D-dimer
  • Harms: bleeding history, renal impairment, antiplatelet therapy, frailty, patient preference and likelihood of follow-up
Muscular is not trivial; muscular is not automatically anticoagulated. UIP guidance allows surveillance for isolated gastrocnemius/soleal clot in selected patients. A high-risk or symptomatic muscular clot can still warrant anticoagulation. Avoid converting an anatomical label into an automatic treatment rule. [2]

How to word a report

Good: “Right medial gastrocnemius intramuscular vein non-compressible over approximately 4 cm, proximal thrombus tip approximately 3 cm distal to popliteal confluence. Popliteal and femoral veins compressible. Peroneal veins technically incompletely seen. Findings suspicious for isolated muscular calf DVT; comprehensive vascular duplex advised for mapping.”

Unsafe: “DVT negative” when only femoral/popliteal sites or a subset of calf branches have been scanned.

4. The small saphenous: a superficial vein with a serious junction

Decision-critical question: Where is the proximal end of the SVT relative to the true saphenopopliteal junction, and has thrombus crossed into the popliteal vein?
  1. Find the SSV in its posterior calf fascial compartment; track it proximally all the way to the junction or document variant drainage.
  2. Measure clot length and proximal tip-to-SPJ distance using the vessel course; assess the popliteal vein with compression.
  3. For lower-limb SVT within 3 cm of a deep junction, ESVS recommends therapeutic-dose anticoagulation (consensus-level evidence); duration is individualised, often around 3 months when treated as junctional high-risk disease. [6]
  4. For longer SVT >5 cm and sufficiently remote from deep junctions, fondaparinux 2.5 mg daily for 45 days has strong trial/guideline support; rivaroxaban 10 mg daily for 45 days is an alternative in some guidelines. Confirm local approvals and contraindications. [7]
  5. Important nuance: US 2023 consensus statement is explicitly about SVT ≤3 cm from the SFJ; applying a simple 3 cm threshold to the SPJ is supported more directly by the ESVS “deep vein junction” guidance and expert extrapolation, with more uncertainty owing to anatomical variation. [6] [7]

Do not mistake a superficial SSV clot near the knee for a gastrocnemius deep vein clot. The fascia, course, and confluence tell you what you are seeing.

5. ABOVE THE KNEE — the essential ED compression study

The proximal study is more reproducible and more accurate than calf POCUS, but a limited scan is a protocol, not a whole-leg clearance certificate.

  1. Common femoral vein (CFV): transverse at groin, above and below great saphenous inflow; compress fully. Inspect saphenofemoral junction and deep femoral/femoral split.
  2. Femoral vein: follow the vessel through proximal, mid and distal thigh with overlapping short-axis compressions. Remember “superficial femoral vein” is a misleading obsolete name — it is a deep vein.
  3. Adductor canal: consider alternative windows if the distal femoral vein is difficult to compress.
  4. Popliteal vein: knee flexed, posterior approach; compress down to the below-knee transition/tibioperoneal outflow as allowed by your validated protocol.
  5. Iliac caveat: proximal pelvic/iliac thrombosis may be missed by compression of accessible segments; whole-leg swelling, disproportionate symptoms or abnormal common femoral Doppler phasicity should prompt further imaging.
ED two-region POCUS versus whole-leg ultrasound: a two-region study can answer “Is there a proximal DVT at the sites assessed?” It cannot answer “Is there any DVT anywhere in this leg?” If a limited study is negative but concern remains, use an established D-dimer/pre-test probability pathway with repeat proximal ultrasound (commonly around 5–7 days) or formal whole-leg duplex. Follow the hospital's locally approved pathway.

7. Evidence that changes practice

CACTUS trial (2016)

259 enrolled, 252 in ITT analysis; selected low-risk symptomatic isolated calf DVT. LMWH versus placebo: composite proximal VTE outcome 3% vs 5%, not statistically significant; bleeding 4% vs 0%. Underpowered and not generalisable to cancer/prior VTE/high-risk patients. [9]

RIDTS trial (2022)

402 randomised after 6 initial weeks rivaroxaban; 6 extra weeks versus placebo: recurrent VTE 11% vs 19% at follow-up; mainly recurrent distal DVT; proximal DVT/PE 3% vs 4%, not significant. Not evidence every calf clot must be treated. [10]

CHEST 2021

For isolated distal DVT without severe symptoms/risk factors, serial imaging for 2 weeks preferred over routine anticoagulation (conditional). For higher-risk cases, anticoagulation favoured; if treated, use full-dose DVT regimen. [3]

UIP 2023/2024 summary

Emphasises symptomatic burden, location (axial versus muscular), extension risk and bleeding risk. Intramuscular clot can be surveilled in suitable low-risk patients. [2]

Local application: NZ practice

It is defensible to actively look for and document gastrocnemius/soleal thrombosis. It does not follow that every such clot requires anticoagulation. This page does not establish a unique national NZ protocol; before clinical deployment, align imaging scope, reporting terminology, repeat-scan access and drug choices with Whangārei/Health NZ vascular radiology and thrombosis guidance.

Practice questions for trainees

1. One thrombosed gastrocnemius vein, popliteal normal, low symptom burden, recent ankle injury. Anticoagulate?

Not automatically. Confirm extent with formal duplex if this was a limited POCUS study; assess extension risk, bleeding risk and follow-up ability. Serial scan weekly for 2 weeks may be appropriate in lower-risk patients under local pathway.

2. SSV clot tip 1 cm from the demonstrated SPJ, popliteal patent. Ignore because superficial?

No. This is junctional high-risk SVT. Full duplex to map extent; ESVS guidance supports therapeutic anticoagulation for SVT ≤3 cm from a deep junction, with patient-specific bleeding assessment.

3. Groin and popliteal two-region scan negative; calf pain and positive D-dimer. DVT excluded?

No. Follow validated pretest-probability/serial-imaging or whole-leg-duplex pathway. The examined sites are negative, not the whole leg.

4. Anterior tibial DVT — not worth recording?

Incorrect. It is an axial deep calf DVT even if uncommon and technically harder to identify. Map and manage according to symptoms/extension risk.
